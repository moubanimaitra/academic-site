Moubani Maitra

I am a writer and social science researcher currently based in Zürich, Switzerland. My research lies at the intersection of critical political economy, the anthropology of the state, and citizen-state relations in South Asia. Drawing on several years of long-term ethnographic fieldwork, I examine how citizenship and democracy have evolved in contemporary India.

My PhD dissertation explores transformations in the Indian political economy since 2014, when the Bharatiya Janata Party (BJP) came to power. In particular, I focus on urban governance, land dispossession, civil society, public service delivery, and welfare policies.

I ground my analysis in empirical research while situating it within broader theoretical debates in political anthropology, political science, development studies, and urban studies. More broadly, I am interested in understanding the role of state institutions in a democracy: the interests and roles of state and non-state actors, the relationships between them, and the ways in which their interactions and institutional practices may contribute to democratic backsliding.

Beyond my academic work, I have increasingly found myself drawn to multimedia projects and alternative forms of storytelling. I am keen to explore these further as ways of making my research accessible to wider audiences, particularly through documentary filmmaking and visual storytelling using photography and sound.

Research

  • 01
    The Paradox of Direct Benefit Transfers: Rent-Seeking, Brokerage, and the New Middlemen in India’s Urban Housing Scheme Working paper Read abstract Hide abstract

    Following the BJP’s electoral victories in 2014, 2019, and 2024, its success has often been attributed to the implementation of welfare schemes. Central to these schemes is digitalisation and the promise of unmediated delivery through Direct Benefit Transfers (DBTs), a defining feature of what scholars term the BJP’s “New Welfarism.” This model combines the provision of goods such as housing, toilets, and gas connections through centrally sponsored schemes with digital technologies that facilitate direct transfers to beneficiaries’ bank accounts. The figure of the labharthi (beneficiary) has become central to welfare politics, reinforcing narratives that credit Prime Minister Narendra Modi for welfare delivery. This paper examines the Pradhan Mantri Awas Yojana (Urban) [PMAY(U)], a flagship housing scheme employing geo-tagging and staged cash transfers, to explore how applicants access welfare and become beneficiaries through interactions with state and non-state actors. Grounded in long-term ethnographic fieldwork in Uttar Pradesh, a key site of BJP governance, it examines a market-oriented model in which beneficiaries must mobilise resources and connections to complete house construction.

    Despite digitalisation and DBTs, brokerage continues to shape access, particularly for landless applicants, with local brokers and elected representatives mediating approvals. DBTs have also produced new intermediaries who extract rents, challenging claims of eliminating middlemen. Rather than expanding state capacity, digital welfare incorporates unelected private actors into delivery, diffusing accountability and creating new avenues for rent-seeking. These dynamics make access to formal entitlements contingent on political connections or payments, undermining democratic citizenship.

  • 02
    Reconfiguring Civil Society: Institutional Constraints and Democratic Backsliding in Contemporary India Working paper Read abstract Hide abstract

    Civil society constitutes an essential pillar of democracy, performing a critical oversight function by holding state power accountable and enabling democratic participation. It encompasses a diverse range of actors and forms of engagement, including social movements, activism, trade unions, and non-governmental organisations (NGOs). However, existing scholarship also highlights that civil society is not inherently democratic and may, at times, advance non-democratic ideals and agendas. Drawing on ethnographic fieldwork, this paper identifies three interconnected arenas of civil society engagement: NGO-led movements, Right to Information (RTI) activism, and collective action, and examines how the institutional space within which civil society operates and performs its democratic oversight function has been significantly reconfigured in contemporary India.

    It identifies three key mechanisms underpinning this transformation: the introduction and arbitrary application of ambiguous funding regulations governing NGOs; changes in the institutional and procedural frameworks of RTI, alongside increased surveillance and intimidation of RTI activists; and the deployment of state coercive apparatuses, particularly the police, to constrain protests and collective mobilisation. The paper argues that these developments collectively signal a shrinking institutional and political space for civil society, undermining opportunities for democratic participation and accountability. It situates these transformations within the broader trajectory of democratic backsliding in contemporary India.

    “Everything has been privatised”: Waste Management and the Politics of Public-Private Partnerships in Urban India Working paper Read abstract Hide abstract

    This paper examines the political economy of public-private partnership (PPP) restructuring at the municipal level through the case of the Solid Waste Management (SWM) component of the Swachh Bharat Mission, launched by the BJP government. It explores how the increasing reliance on PPPs in public service delivery, particularly in waste management and sanitation, has reshaped capital-labour-state relations. The Swachh Bharat Mission, with its high-profile popular campaign, has further consolidated the PPP model as a dominant mode of organising public service provision. In the municipality under study, located in a tier-II city in Uttar Pradesh, the outsourcing of municipal waste management to multiple private companies through PPP arrangements has emerged as the principal mechanism for addressing the city’s waste management crisis. This model has also become the dominant mode of recruiting and employing sanitation workers (safai karamcharis), who overwhelmingly belong to marginalised caste backgrounds. Drawing on field observations, interviews, government documents, and audit reports, this paper argues that the turn towards PPPs cannot be understood simply as a consequence of inadequate state capacity. Rather, it represents a reconfiguration and relocation of state capacity towards private companies, with the state increasingly assuming the role of contract manager instead of expanding its own public-sector workforce and institutional capacity to deliver waste management services. This shift prioritises the management of relations with capital while distancing the state from its direct responsibilities towards labour. The result is a political economy in which public funds and service-delivery responsibilities are channelled through private intermediaries, while the costs of fiscal constraints and contractual failures are disproportionately borne by outsourced sanitation workers.

    Displaced from the city, Deleted from the Vote: Evictions and the Production of Electoral Exclusion in India Working paper Read abstract Hide abstract

    Evictions and home demolitions have increased significantly across India in recent years. According to the Housing and Land Rights Network (HLRN, 2024), approximately 500,000 people were forcibly evicted and nearly 100,000 homes demolished in 2023, the highest annual figure since documentation began in 2016. Uttar Pradesh has witnessed a similar trend, with successive HLRN reports indicating a steady rise in demolitions since 2017. Against this backdrop, the bulldozer has emerged as a prominent political symbol in the rhetoric and election campaigns of BJP politicians, particularly in Uttar Pradesh.

    Drawing on the eviction of a Muslim-majority neighbourhood in Lucknow, the capital of Uttar Pradesh, this paper examines eviction across three phases: before, during, and after displacement. It argues that eviction cannot be understood merely as material and spatial displacement, or solely through the frameworks of ethno-nationalist politics and neoliberal urban restructuring. Rather, it is a broader political process involving legal legitimisation, the production of residents’ compliance, and the consolidation of new forms of exclusion. The paper examines how courts and legal institutions legitimised the eviction, how residents’ compliance was manufactured through coercion, infrastructural attrition, and intimidation, and how subsequent relocation to the urban periphery through a housing scheme produced an ostensibly welfarist outcome.

    The paper demonstrates that despite following formal legal procedures and providing alternative housing, the eviction resulted in the political disenfranchisement of the displaced population. Alongside their spatial relocation, residents’ names were deleted from electoral rolls during the Special Intensive Revision (SIR) exercise. The case thus reveals how legal and welfare-oriented interventions can simultaneously facilitate displacement and electoral exclusion, raising critical questions about the relationship between urban dispossession, citizenship, and democracy.

Courses Taught

Critical Perspectives on Urban India

Course Curator & Lecturer · Fall 2026

Course Description

This course offers a critical, ethnographically grounded introduction to urban India, examining the social, political, and economic dynamics that produce inequality and exclusion in Indian cities. Key themes include the formal-informal divide, informal settlements, land rights and housing, precarious labour and the gig economy, welfare politics, the urban middle class, and questions of environment, gender, and citizenship. Drawing on case studies from Delhi, Mumbai, Hyderabad, and Bengaluru, the course situates these debates within specific political economy contexts while enabling comparative insight across urban India. It is suited to students in urban studies, anthropology, sociology, development studies, and social policy.
